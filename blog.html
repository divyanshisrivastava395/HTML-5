My First Hackathon

A raw, honest diary of 8 days building a meme-powered dodge game — surviving bugs, bad code, and sleepless nights at Code Clutch.

📅 8 Days 🎮 Game: DodgeLife 🏆 Top 21 of 107 Teams

I signed up for my very first hackathon armed with nothing but a rough idea and no real strategy. The next 8 days turned into a whirlwind of sleepless nights, endless debugging sessions, AI-generated rabbit holes, and moments I'll never forget. This is that journey — told exactly the way I lived it, straight from my diary.

107 Total Teams
Top 21 We Reached
8 Days to Build
01 The Spark
Saturday — Day 1

I registered for the hackathon and immediately started searching for ideas. I read through rule books and concept guides, and learned we had to build a 2D game — something simple, not too complex, but with some interactive value.

My first thought was a Space Waves-style shooter. Then I started thinking about it differently — what if I connected the game concept to something real? Engineering student obstacles: assignments, exams, attendance. That felt personal. That felt fun.

💡 Initial idea: Space Waves → Pivot: obstacles as real-life engineering student struggles
02 The Crash
Sunday — Day 2

I tried to build it. It failed miserably. I couldn't figure out how to put different obstacles together — it was either way too easy or not difficult at all. Nothing was working.

Then my friend suggested the game should work for all age groups, not just engineering students. That made sense. But I still had no solution to the core problem.

I asked ChatGPT. It mentioned something called a dodger game — I had no idea what that was. I didn't know the meme, I didn't know the format, I didn't know how to scale it. That was the end of day two — confused, but somehow still going.

😅 First real failure. Didn't even know what a dodger game was.
02→ The Pivot to Memes
Sunday (continued)

I researched what kind of trouble every individual faces in their daily life — what makes them want to dodge obstacles? That question led to the real insight: it's not just engineering problems. It's life distractions, daily troubles, things that stop you from being successful.

So it became a dodge game — you dodge obstacles (emojis) that represent everyday struggles. On that day, I built the basic game idea, applied emojis to the obstacles, and got many "fail trails" from ChatGPT to refine it.

03 The Meme Hunt
Monday — Day 3

Now the game needed more life — more fun. I thought: what if hitting an obstacle triggered a meme? The idea was brilliant in my head. The execution? Not so much.

I couldn't find how to download the right MP3 files. It took 3–4 hours just to figure out the audio format. I tried many approaches. Eventually I reached the conclusion: after hitting an obstacle, a specific meme image would be generated — and since it was too silent, I decided to add meme sounds too.

Finding audio and downloading everything took a full day. I listened to more than 100 meme sounds and saw more than 200 meme pictures — keeping correct names, copying them into folders, checking many versions of the same sound.

🎵 Day 3: Became a full-time meme archivist. 100+ sounds. 200+ images. No regrets.
04 The Boosters Problem
Day 4

I read in the rulebook that a game should have some extra flair. I thought about adding boosters — like coffee, headphones, and food items — that give you extra life when collected. I had the idea and even had a sound ready for it, but couldn't apply it that day.

I gave what I had to my brother to play-test. His feedback? The boosters were spawning way too much — it was almost impossible to lose. So I reduced the numbers.

I tried to get help from ChatGPT for the JavaScript code. Got many failed codes. Sometimes I had to write entire sections from scratch again.

05 The Time System
Day 5

I read that a game should be about 3 minutes. So I thought — why not make it feel like a full day? I designed a time system: first 30 seconds = 4am–12pm (dawn to afternoon), next 30 seconds = 12pm–8pm (afternoon to night), last 30 seconds = 8pm–4am (night to dawn).

I updated the UI and background to change according to the time of day. First I tried direct changes, then refined it further. The background evolving with your progress made the game feel alive.

🌅 The day-night cycle was the detail that made people stop and say "wait, that's cool."
06 Sound Wars
Day 6

Today I tried to connect the meme sounds properly. Some of them I had to cut. I only needed a part of each — then I needed a glowing shield mechanic for the headphone booster: when collected, the player glows and is protected, but the shield disappears after 5 seconds or after hitting any 5 obstacles — and stops glowing when either of those happens. Lives are limited to 3, coffee boosts your speed, and after every 5 seconds of appearing, boosters disappear and new ones spawn in new places.

The hardest part: background music. I needed the background sound to stop when all lives are lost — that's when the meme sound kicks in. And the meme sound had to stop the moment "Play Again" is clicked. First attempt: tried stopping background music when a booster was collected — bad idea. The logic kept breaking.

Got too many wrong codes from ChatGPT. Sometimes I had to write the whole thing from scratch again.

🔊 Pro tip I learned: never fully trust AI-generated code for audio state management. Always verify.
07 The Final Polish
Day 7

Did all the last minimal changes to the game, then did the online presentation. I made it perfect and submitted it to the judges. We got selected for the offline round! The judges were so happy about the sound and the original PPT — we made our presentation even more perfect with the feedback we received.

08 The Offline Round
Day 8 — The Big Day

We traveled about 30 km to the offline venue to present DodgeLife: Survive the Chaos. It wasn't perfect — we panicked over which laptop we'd play the game on, and it wasn't working properly at first.

But in the end, we adjusted everything and gave our presentation to the HOD, who praised us. Then we gave the PPT presentation to the judges, followed by a live game demo.

The turning point: out of the last 21 finalists, only 7 were selected. We were not in it. I felt sad — but I learned something.

It's not necessary to always win. What matters is the process — and how far we got at that point.

What This Week Was Really About

  • We started this hackathon with just an idea — to build something simple, relatable and fun.
  • Through brainstorming and planning, we developed our concept into a 2D game called DodgeLife: Survive the Chaos.
  • The process involved continuous coding, debugging, and improving features like gameplay, UI, and sound.
  • We faced multiple challenges — bugs, design issues, and time pressure — but solved them step by step.
  • We successfully presented our project online, explaining our idea and giving a live demo.
  • Out of 107 teams, we reached top 21 finalists — which was a proud achievement.
  • Although we didn't make it to the top 7, the experience itself was incredibly valuable.
  • We learned technical skills, problem-solving, and how important polish and presentation are.
"We didn't lose — we just discovered how to win next time. This was not the end of a hackathon. It was the beginning of a mindset where I don't stop until I reach the top." — From my diary, Day 8
